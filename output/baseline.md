**The Setting**

Mist clung to the moss-draped boughs of the elderwood like the faded memory of some ancient sorrow. Dawn crept reluctantly over the horizon, its pallid light filtered through a lattice of branches that had stood witness to centuries of secrets—secrets I once carried like smuggled embers beneath my cloak. The air smelled of wet loam and the faint spice of autumn decay, familiar scents that called back the long-buried days of my youth. My boots pressed softly into the forest floor, each step stirring the chill fog, swirling it like restless spirits given momentary form.

I paused upon a knotted root, letting the cold seep through the leather of my gloves and into my bones. Age had thinned my hair and gentled my limbs, but it had failed to dull the sharpness of memory. It was here, in this very wood—when my body was lithe and my heart reckless—that I had first traced forbidden sigils in the dirt. The quill of the necromancer had been placed in my trembling hand by moonlight alone, for the teaching of such arts was then an offense whispered only of in locked chambers.

Yet now, decades later, necromancy had become no more alarming to the common folk than a blacksmith’s forge or a midwife’s charm. Entire academies stood proudly upon hilltops, their towers etched with runic blessings that I had once copied onto scraps of stolen parchment. Young scholars walked brazenly through city gates with bone-wands dangling at their belts, their robes marked with the sanctioned crest of the Collegium Mortis.

And still—still—the world dared to condemn my blood.

My grandchild. My bright, inquisitive Amara, whose mind shimmered with curiosity like sunlit water. She sat now in a cold cell of the High Tribunal, accused not of practice—but of *interest*. Interest in demon summoning. A field that, in my own wild youth, had been little more than another branch of the arcane—a discipline neither praised nor feared, but regarded with the same wary respect as fire, cliffside herbs, or the weathered runes of the First Tongue.

The injustice festered beneath my ribs.

**The Action**

I drew a long breath and allowed the forest’s silence to speak. Ravens croaked overhead, wings cracking the morning hush like brittle parchment. Somewhere in the distance, water coursed over stone in a steady, murmuring chant—a song older than any city tribunal. It reminded me of the nights when I had first studied binding-circles, the ink glowing faintly as my mentor, Master Tolvern, murmured: *“Life and death are threads of one loom. Respect the weave, and it will respect you.”*

Had he known then how the world would twist such teachings?

I began the slow walk toward the city walls. Their silhouette rose between the trees like the jagged spine of a slumbering colossus, turquoise banners fluttering from the battlements. Elders in the capital claimed those banners represented purity, but I knew better. I had been among the few who stitched them in my youth—threads dyed not with symbolism, but with practicality, chosen simply because the color wore well against smoke and rain. The city had always been a place of stories, but not always of truth.

As I approached the gate, guards clad in gleaming splint-mail crossed their halberds before my path. Their armor smelled faintly of oiled metal and the acrid tang of last night's torch smoke.

“Halt,” one barked. “The Tribunal is in session. No entry without writ.”

I let my gaze drift to the horizon behind them, watching the sun stain the sky in shades of fire and brass. My heart tightened, as though gripped by an unseen hand. I had no writ. What I possessed was something far older—something the Tribunal had forgotten in their comfort and self-righteousness.

“I seek only to speak truth,” I said softly.

The guards exchanged glances, uncertain. Then, as fate often does, the moment shifted. A gale swept through the gatehouse, stirring dust and loose parchment into a brief cyclone. The temperature dropped abruptly. Frost crawled along the stones like white veins.

The guards’ eyes widened. “Necromancy!” one cried, gripping his halberd with both hands.

“No,” I answered. “Memory.”

Because the power that welled up from my palms was not death-magic, but rather the deep imprint of oaths I had carved into my very soul decades ago—the same oaths that had allowed me to walk unhindered among spirits and echoes of the past.

The frost crystallized into faint shapes—faces from forgotten times, mentors and friends who had once wielded demonology as scholars, not criminals. They did not speak, but their presence sang louder than any words.

The guards fell back. And I walked past them.

Through the stone corridors I strode, the cool air brushing my cheeks like whispered warnings. My footsteps echoed—a slow, deliberate cadence of judgment. At the grand doors of the Tribunal chamber, the scent of incense struck me—sharp, medicinal, masking the more honest smells of fear and politics.

Inside, the robed judges turned toward me as though I were a specter. Amara stood in chains upon a small dais, her grey eyes widening in disbelief.

“Grandfather?” she breathed.

“The charges against her,” I declared, “are hypocrisy woven into law.”

Whispers rustled through the chamber like dry leaves in a storm.

“You dare interrupt the Tribunal?” barked the High Magistrate, his ceremonial staff ringing against the marble floor.

“I dare remind you,” I said, voice low as thunder on the horizon, “that once I was condemned for what you now praise. And you will not damn the next generation for what you yourselves have forgotten.”

I raised my hands, not in threat, but in invocation. The memories once more swirled around me—visions of academies where demonology was a respected discipline, where summoners were healers of the psyche and binders of malignant spirits. Where the term “demon” referred not to evil incarnate, but to ancient intelligences consulted for wisdom.

The illusions filled the chamber—old classrooms illuminated by floating lanterns, teachers inscribing glyphs in shimmering chalk, young scholars debating the ethics of invocation.

Amara watched with parted lips, awe softening her fear.

The judges stared at the visions, their faces draining of color as generations of revisionism crumbled before their eyes.

**The Aftermath**

When the last echo of memory faded, silence engulfed the hall. The Magistrate lowered his staff, its clatter against the marble muted by the weight of revelation.

“We… were not taught this,” he whispered.

“Then learn,” I answered. “Let her learn. Let the truth breathe again.”

The chains fell from Amara’s wrists, clattering like spent lies upon the floor. She ran to me, her small hands clutching my cloak, her hair carrying the scent of the cold stone cell she had endured.

I touched her cheek, feeling the warmth of youth—a warmth I had long thought I no longer deserved to feel.

Outside, the bells of the city began to toll. Not in alarm, but in transformation. Their notes rolled across rooftops, through market squares, and into every cramped alley where old stories slept under the dust of new laws.

Amara looked up at me, eyes bright. “Will things change now?”

I gazed toward the open doors of the Tribunal, where sunlight streamed in golden shafts across the floor.

“They always do,” I murmured. “But only when someone remembers what was forgotten.”

And together we stepped into the dawn, leaving behind the fading scent of incense, the last traces of frost, and a world beginning, at last, to unshackle its own history.
